Copy variations, and what each one bets on

Eight hero treatments for the same product. They are not eight ways of saying one thing — each assumes something different about who is reading and what they already believe. The reasoning matters more than the lines.

The gate

Market → offer → copy, answered honestly

Copy is the last variable and the weakest one. Before choosing a line, these three have to be answered — and two of them currently can't be.

1 · Who is starving?

Designers and design engineers who already brief coding agents daily, and who have already paid for something to reduce that friction. We have no list, no buyers, and no evidence anyone has paid to solve this.

What we do know: Aloud shipped this free. Someone else believed the hunger existed — and priced it at zero.

Invented

2 · What's the proposition?

A free beta of a Mac app that turns a spoken review into an agent-ready brief. No price, so no risk to reverse, and no revenue to learn from.

A free tool can't tell you whether the problem was worth money. It can only tell you whether it was worth installing.

Thin

3 · What's the proof?

One real number exists: this page's own feedback round was captured with the tool in a single pass, and the three notes it produced were specific enough to act on without a follow-up question.

That's a demo, not a result. No time saved has been measured against a baseline.

One anecdote

So: the correct output here is not a finished headline. It's a set of bets, each of which names the reader it needs, so the right one can be chosen by contact instead of taste.

The axes

What a hero line can lead with

Promise

What changes for you. Works on a reader who feels the pain but hasn't seen a fix. Weakest when the category is crowded — every tool promises speed.

Mechanism

How it works, as news. Works on a sophisticated reader who has already discounted promises and now only believes a method.

Enemy

Names the status quo you're replacing. Cheap recognition, and it picks a fight you have to win in the next scroll.

Identity

Who you become by using it. Highest ceiling, lowest floor — lands hard on the right reader, reads as posturing to everyone else.

Your audience sits at stage 3–4 sophistication: they have seen the promises and stopped believing them. That pushes the good money toward mechanism and enemy, and away from promise.

What to do

The cheapest way to find out

Choosing between these by taste is the failure mode. Four moves, in order, none of which need the product to be finished:

  1. Measure a real pass. Count notes and elapsed time across ten reviews you'd do anyway. That kills or confirms variation 04, and gives every other line a real number to use.
  2. Post variation 02 alone — no product, no link. If people reply with their own version of the story, the enemy is real and the market exists.
  3. Send 08 as a subject line to twenty people who already brief agents. Opens tell you about the market; replies tell you about the offer.
  4. Only then write the page. Whichever bet survives contact becomes the headline, and the rest become the section heads further down.

Note that none of this is a copy problem. The page is currently running variation 01, which is the weakest bet on this list for a stage 3–4 reader — and no amount of rewriting fixes a line that's aimed at a reader who isn't there.
